译者按语：Mulata是一位在泰缅边境长大的土瓦族（Dawei）写作者。她跟我描述家乡高当（Kawthaung），说那里的土壤很咸，只能种橡胶树。镇上唯一提供就业机会的是一个制冰厂，供应到泰国的渔业工厂。很长一段时间，这里没有高中，年轻人读完初中（其实很多人也不读）就去泰国打工。当然，大部分也就是去渔业工厂。
<br>
到2008年左右，高当才有了第一所公立高中，但人们认为与其去念这个书不如去泰国打工，毕竟，在一个充斥功能饮料和ဖန်စီဒိုင်း（某种麻醉药）混合的劣质毒品校舍里能学到什么呢？于是这里变得像一个“鬼镇”，年轻人和中年人纷纷离开，只剩下老人。
<br>
这里离缅甸权力中心太远，电力往南最远只送到孟邦，无法企及高当。但这里离泰国近，于是人们一直用泰国电力。也因为地理位置，这里是著名的黑市。从奈温时代起，军政府反复封锁边贸路线，以至于人们的日常用品诸如味精、食用油、服装、护肤品、护肤品都需要从泰国走私。
<br>
这些描述都让我想到，在高当，她父亲作为1988年的大学生，会有多难得。于是请她写下父亲的故事。
</p>

在地图软件上看**高当（Kawthaung）**。缅甸-泰国边境是一条蓝色实线。这条线是巴占河（Pakchan River）。从电脑屏幕看的话，河水宁静而湛蓝。但如果你将地图时间调到2012年11月，画面就不一样了。

一只渡船抵达河对岸，大概需要40分钟。这是连接两个世界的短暂旅程。对谷歌地图来说，这不过是一个普通的渡河点。但对当地人来说，这条河是唯一的生存、贸易、甚至逃生的路线。

{% include post-figure.html src="/assets/img/uploads/14/01.jpg" caption="谷歌地图显示了近岸水域边界以及流经高当和拉廊之间的巴占河。" %}

**返乡后的故事**

现在，先在地图上往北挪800公里。你会看见仰光大学。1988年，这个校园里充斥着奋起疾呼的学生，正在为民主而战。一个聪慧勇敢的年轻人（我的父亲）跻身其中，也因此被迫付出代价。当局停止了他的学业，将他投进监狱。在同年获释之后，他被送往家乡，这个国家的边缘：高当。

{% include post-figure.html src="/assets/img/uploads/14/05.gif" %}

回到家乡后的父亲，很快就去看望他的老朋友。却发现朋友一家人挤在一个逼仄的出租屋里。问起来才知道，有一天，士兵们闯进了朋友原来的家，强占了房子，然后把自己的军属搬了进去。在那个年代，军队可以为所欲为，普通百姓却因为恐惧而不敢反抗。但父亲无法忍受这种鬼祟的暴行。

他径直冲到房子前，与士兵们当面对质。邻居们被他的勇气所鼓舞，纷纷聚集在他周围，形成一道强大的保护屏障。士兵们看到全镇团结起来，怒不可遏，但在众人的力量面前，他们最终还是退缩了，搬出了房子。

父亲留在了高当，在河边过着平静充实的生活。他以驾驶长尾船谋生，每天在河道上航行。同时，他也把自己的生命献给社区，和朋友们成立了一个小型本地慈善团体。他们购买并收集氧气瓶，免费借给镇上任何呼吸困难的病人。后来，他们还帮助失去亲人的家属安排免费的葬礼。每当小镇遭遇灾害，比如暴雨引发的山体滑坡，父亲和他的朋友们总是第一时间赶到现场进行救援。他因此深受邻里爱戴，只要社区需要帮助，他总是义不容辞。

从小到大，我耳濡目染父亲所行之事，记得家里堆放着沉重的氧气瓶，等待着需要它们的人。对镇上的人来说，他是个值得信赖的人；对我而言，他是一个温暖、令人安心的存在，他教会了我什么是爱邻如己。在他的庇护下，我感到无比安全。

{% include post-figure.html src="/assets/img/uploads/14/06.png" caption="谷歌街景·高当" %}

今天从*谷歌街景*看高当城，会看见树荫之下的房屋，宁静安详。但地图软件无法呈现38年前的那一天，无法呈现身着绿色制服的士兵，也无法呈现那站在门廊上的年轻人，如何拒绝恐惧和服从。电脑数据库只记录了一些形状和房屋，但当地居民还记得那个年轻人的勇气。士兵们悻悻离开，也记住了他的脸。

他们等了很多年，直到一个复仇的机会到来。

**中午12点：正午烈日**

2010年以后，边境线因贸易而热闹起来。从这张老卫星照片就可以看出，河岸冒出了许多新仓库，检查站扩建了，1988年的那些士兵也回来了。他们年纪大了，当上了边检官员。

{% include post-figure.html src="/assets/img/uploads/14/02.jpg" caption="2010 年 12 月 25 日的 Google Earth 历史卫星图像，记录了高当沿河地区不断增长的基础设施和蓬勃发展的贸易。" %}

巴占河是父亲的生计所在。他每天都驾着长尾船穿梭于河面，往返于边境之间，运送货物和人员。由于每次航行都要过界，无法避免地要在码头接受边防官员检查。2012年11月的一个炎热中午，这个检查站却成了他的陷阱。那天，我父亲和那些士兵重逢了。

他们彼此对视的那一刻，空气都凝固了。原来并非陌生人，正是几十年前父亲在朋友家门廊上遇到的那些士兵。他们从未忘记他的脸，也从未原谅他当年在众人面前让他们退缩。

父亲当时正穿着一件救生衣，但没有把那根小带子系在腿上。在官方规则手册中，这只是一个很小的错误。但在边境检查站，这成了士兵们复仇的最佳契机。11月的巴占河水依然湍急，从河岸到码头，冲击出大约30米的河滩。

{% include post-figure.html src="/assets/img/uploads/14/03.jpg" caption="图3: 谷歌地球3D视图，俯瞰烈日下的巴占河，以及高当附近的岛屿，他曾被迫在那里游泳。" %}

在*谷歌地球*上，如果把时间滑到中午12点，所有阴影都会消失。

他们强迫他跳进河里游了一个来回。烈日直射头顶，河面反射着刺眼的白光。卫星摄影拍不到河里的情况，拍不到一个中年男子在酷热中游了几个小时，拍不到持枪监视他的士兵。

他已经筋疲力尽，却依然在烈日下不停地游，仿佛要一直游下去，直到士兵们满意为止。那天，等他终于回到家时，身体才瘫倒在地。他失去了意识，口吐白沫。家人把他送到医院，但为时已晚。他脑中的一根血管已经破裂。

官方死亡证明上写着：脑出血。简单明了的医学术语，它没有提到烈日暴晒、湍急的河水，也没有提到士兵们的仇恨。

几乎全镇的人都来参加了葬礼。即使官方文件什么都没说，他们也都知道他真正的死因。那天，我站在人群中，看那些他曾帮助过的人们流着眼泪，感到无比沉重。我意识到，父亲践行了他一直教导我们的事。即使现在，每当我看到我们这条河的电子地图，我看到的不仅仅是坐标，而是仿佛看到父亲掌握船舵的手。我心中涌起深远而平静的承诺：我们必须像他那样，带着勇气继续活下去。

{% include post-figure.html src="/assets/img/uploads/14/04.png" caption="父亲的肖像 （由ai根据照片制作）" %}

**同样的模式**

如今，人们在新闻中看见的缅甸，充斥着硝烟和血色战痕，往往以为这些暴力冲突是2021年2月政变之后的事。

但对我来说，新闻里的东西是我久违又熟悉的沉重。2012年悄悄夺走父亲生命的酷刑，正是今天无数家庭正在面对的阴影。这种痛苦在世间却无迹可寻。

如果你在夜里点开高当的地图，屏幕上会是漆黑一片，只有岸边零星几户闪着昏黄灯光。河水静谧。但只有当那些怪物彻底消失，当人们能真正安然入睡时，这地图才算得上完整。

**The 40-minute boat ride**

*By/Maluta*

Look at Kawthaung on a digital map. The border between Myanmar and Thailand is just a solid blue line. This line is the Pakchan River. On a computer screen, the water looks perfectly smooth and blue. But if you change the calendar back to November 2012, the picture changes.

A boat ride across this river takes around forty minutes. It is a short trip between two different worlds. For Google maps, it is just a normal place for boats. But for the local people, this water is the only way to survive, trade, or even escape.

Figure 1: Map view from Google Maps showing the close water border and the Pakchan River flowing between Kawthaung and Ranong

**The story of a forced return**

Now, move your map 800 kilometers north. You will find University of Rangoon. In 1988, this campus was full of loud, brave students. They were fighting for democracy.

One student there was an intelligent young man (my father). The government punished him. They stopped his education, put him in prison.They banished him to the his hometown, the absolute edge of the country: Kawthaung.

When my father finally arrived back in his small hometown, he went to visit his old friends. He was heartbroken to find his friend's entire family squeezed into a tiny, cramped rented room. When he asked them what happened to their beautiful home, they told him the painful truth: soldiers had simply walked in, seized the property, and moved their own military families right into it. In those days, the army could take whatever they wanted, and ordinary people were too terrified to speak up. But my father refused to accept this quiet cruelty.

He marched right up to the house to confront the soldiers face-to-face. Seeing his bravery, the neighbors in the community suddenly felt inspired. They gathered around him, forming a large, protective crowd to back him up. The soldiers were furious to see the whole neighborhood standing together, but faced with the power of the crowd, they finally backed down and moved out of the house.

After reclaiming the home, my father stayed in Kawthaung and built a quiet, purposeful life on the water. He worked as a long-tail boat driver, navigating the river every day to earn a living. But his true life was dedicated to the community. He and his friends started a small local charity group. His group bought and collected oxygen cylinders and loaned them out completely for free to anyone in the town who was sick and struggling to breathe. Later, they helped grieving families arrange free funeral services. Whenever the town faced a disaster like the heavy monsoon rains that triggered sudden landslides from the hills, my father and his friends were always the first to run and sort out. He was loved by his neighbourhood, always answering whenever the community called for help.

Growing up, I watched my father do all of this. I remember seeing the heavy oxygen tanks stacked in our home, waiting for someone who needed them. To the town, he was a reliable man, and to me, he is a warm, safe presence who showed me what it meant to love your neighbours. I felt so safe under his shadow.

If you look at the town center on Google street view today, the houses look quiet under the green trees. The digital map does not show what happened in the late 1980s. It does not show the soldiers in green uniforms. It does not show this man standing on a porch, refusing to be afraid. Soldiers had taken his friends' houses, but he forced the soldiers to leave. The computer database only sees regular shapes and houses. But the local people remember his courage. The soldiers left back then, but they remembered his face. They waited many years for a chance to get revenge.

**12:00 PM: The hot midday sun**

By 2010, the border became very busy with trade. You can see this on old satellite photos. New warehouses appeared. The checkpoints grew larger. The same soldiers from 1988 came back. They were older now, and they worked as border officials.

Figure 2: Google Earth historical satellite imagery from December 25, 2010, archiving the growing infrastructure and booming trade along the Kawthaung waterfront.

The Pakchan River was my father's livelihood. Every day, he drove his long-tail boat across the water, moving goods and people between the borders. Because he was crossing the boundary, getting checked by the border officials at the pier was a mandatory routine. He could not avoid them. On a hot day in November 2012, this mandatory checkpoint became a trap. While stepping up for the required inspection, he found himself standing face-to-face with the officials.

When they looked at each other, the air went cold. They were not strangers. These were the exact same soldiers my father had faced down decades ago on that neighborhood porch. They had never forgotten his face, and they had never forgiven him for making them back down in front of the crowd.

The soldiers used a stupid excuse to take their revenge finally. My father was wearing a life jacket, but he did not tie the small strap around his thigh. In an official rulebook, that is a very small mistake. But at the border checkpoint, the soldiers used it to be cruel.

They forced him to swim back and forth across the wide river as a punishment. In November, the water is heavy and the river stretches nearly 0.3 kilometers wide between the shore and the pier.

Figure 3:  Google Earth 3D view looking down at the vast, sunlit waters of the Pakchan River and the islands near Kawthaung where he was forced to swim

On Google Earth, if you move the time slider to 12:00 PM, all the shadows disappear. The sun is directly overhead. The river reflects a blinding white light. The satellite camera cannot see under the water. It cannot see a middle-aged man swimming for hours in the terrible heat. It cannot see the soldiers watching him with guns.

He was incredibly exhausted, but he kept swimming lap after lap under the burning sun: doomed to continue until the soldiers finally felt satisfied and told him he could stop.

When he finally walked inside his home, his body collapsed. He lost consciousness and began foaming at the mouth. His family took him to the hospital, but it was too late. A blood vessel in his brain had already burst.

The official death certificate says: *Cerebral Hemorrhage*. It is a clean, medical phrase. It does not mention the boiling sun, the heavy river water, or the old hatred of the soldiers.

Almost the whole town came to his funeral. Standing in the crowd that day, looking at all the crying faces of the people he had helped, the weight of his life hit me. I realized that my father had truly lived exactly as he taught us to live. Even now, whenever I look at these digital maps of our old river, I don't just see coordinates. I see my father's hands on the boat steering wheel, and I feel a deep, quiet promise in my heart that we must keep living with the same courage he had.

**The same pattern**

When people see Myanmar on the news today, they often think the violence started suddenly with the coup in February 2021. They see new photos of smoke and red war markers on their screens.

But for me, those symbols on the screen carry an old, familiar weight. The quiet cruelty that took my father away in 2012 is the same shadow that so many families across the country are facing right now. It is a pain that leaves an invisible mark on the landscape.

If you look at the map of Kawthaung at night, the screen is dark. You can only see a few yellow lights from local homes on the coast. The river is quiet. The map of this place will only be complete when those monsters are gone, and the people can finally sleep without fear.
